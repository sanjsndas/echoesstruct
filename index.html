The Courtyard House: A Timeless Typology for Modern Living

From ancient Roman domus to contemporary Japanese machiya, the courtyard house has endured because it solves a fundamental problem: how to bring nature, light, and privacy into a dense environment. We trace its evolution and speak to three architects designing courtyards today.

Inside, the courtyard acts as a lung — a breathing space that admits daylight and fresh air while blocking street noise and prying eyes. The material palette is often restrained: stone, timber, gravel, and a single tree. This minimalism is not a style choice but a functional one — every element must earn its place in a space that is both interior and exterior.

Modern interpretations often blur the boundary further. Sliding glass walls, continuous floor finishes, and roofless rooms turn the courtyard into a true outdoor living room. In hotter climates, a shallow reflecting pool or fountain adds evaporative cooling — a low-tech solution that predates air conditioning by millennia.

For architects, the courtyard is a canvas for experimentation with light. The movement of shadows across a wall throughout the day becomes a dynamic artwork. At night, uplighting on a tree or a textured wall transforms the space into a quiet, contemplative retreat.

The Colour of Calm: Pigments That Soothe Rather Than Shout

We are living through a period of chromatic overload. But a quieter palette is emerging — one rooted in earth pigments, chalky finishes, and colours that change with the light. We explore the psychology and craft of calm colour.

Off-whites with a hint of umber. Pale clay pinks. Sage greens that read almost grey. These are not colours that demand attention; they are colours that hold space. Applied in lime wash or mineral paint, they have a soft, suede-like texture that diffuses light rather than reflecting it sharply.

The result is a room that feels enveloping — a refuge from the visual noise of the outside world. Designers like John Pawson and Vincent Van Duysen have long championed this approach, but it is now reaching a broader audience as homeowners seek spaces that support rest and focus.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between interior design and interior architecture? +

Interior design focuses on the aesthetic and functional arrangement of a space — furniture, finishes, colour, and lighting. Interior architecture deals with the structural and spatial modifications of an interior, such as moving walls, adding mezzanines, or reconfiguring circulation. In practice, the two disciplines overlap significantly.

How do I choose the right lighting for a living room? +

Start with ambient light (a dimmable overhead or floor lamp for general illumination), then add task lighting (a reading lamp), accent lighting (to highlight art or architecture), and finally decorative lighting (a sculptural pendant or candles). Layering these four types gives you flexibility for different times of day and moods.

What are the most common space planning mistakes? +

Overlooking circulation paths (leaving less than 90cm for main walkways), placing furniture against every wall, ignoring sightlines from doorways, and underestimating the importance of negative space. Good planning is as much about what you leave out as what you put in.

Is sustainable design more expensive? +

Not necessarily. While some eco-friendly materials carry a premium, many sustainable strategies — passive solar orientation, natural ventilation, reusing existing structures, and choosing durable, low-maintenance finishes — save money over the life of a building. The upfront cost can be comparable to conventional construction.
